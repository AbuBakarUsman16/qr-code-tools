Why Use These QR Code Generators?

QR codes have become the fastest way to move information from a screen or printed page directly into someone's phone. Whether you're sharing a business card at a networking event, giving guests quick access to your home WiFi, or linking customers to your website from a poster, a QR code removes the friction of typing anything by hand.

All of the tools on this site run entirely in your browser. That means your contact details, WiFi password, or text never gets uploaded to a server, the QR code is generated locally on your device, which is faster and more private than most generators you'll find elsewhere.

How to Choose the Right QR Code Type

If you want people to visit a webpage, use the URL generator. If you're handing out a digital business card, the vCard generator saves your full contact info straight into someone's phone contacts with one scan. For cafés, offices, or events where guests need internet access without reading out a password, the WiFi generator is the quickest option. And for anything else, a short message, an address, a code, the plain text generator handles it.

Static codes, and why that matters

Every code generated here is static: the data is encoded in the image itself rather than stored on a server. There is no redirect, no account and no subscription anywhere in the chain.

The practical difference shows up later. A dynamic code from a paid service stops working the moment that service lapses, changes its pricing, or shuts down, and it takes every printed copy with it. Businesses have had menus, packaging and signage go dead this way. A static code has no such failure mode; it will resolve to exactly the same data in ten years. The full comparison is in our guide on static versus dynamic codes.

Getting a code to scan first time

Most QR codes that fail in the real world were sized to fit a layout rather than sized to be scanned. Four things decide whether yours works:

  • Size. A code should be at least one tenth as wide as the distance it is read from, about 2.5cm on a business card, 10cm on a wall poster. Never below 2cm.
  • Contrast. Dark code on a light background. Pale or coloured codes fail unpredictably.
  • Quiet zone. Leave the white margin around the code intact. Cropping to the edge breaks detection.
  • Finish. Matte, not gloss. Glossy lamination reflects overhead light into the camera.

Testing a code before you print it

One test catches most problems, and almost everyone skips it: scan the code on a device that does not already know the answer.

For a WiFi code, that means a phone that has been made to forget the network. A phone already connected will appear to succeed even if your code contains a completely wrong password, it simply reconnects using what it already has, and you learn nothing. For a URL code, test on a device that has never visited the page, since your own browser may be logged in or holding a cached version that hides a problem a first-time visitor will hit.

Then scan it at the real distance, in the real lighting, on an ordinary phone rather than the newest one available. Do this before ordering a print run, not after.
