QR Codes for Cafés and Restaurants: What Actually Works

Four codes cover almost every hospitality use. Most venues print the wrong two.

By Abu Bakar Usman · Published 10 September 2026 · 8 min read

Hospitality adopted QR codes faster than almost any other sector, and then spent several years learning which uses stick and which annoy customers. This is what the evidence of that period suggests, organised by what a venue should actually print.

The four codes worth having

1. WiFi access — the highest value, lowest effort

Staff in a busy café repeat the WiFi password dozens of times a shift. A small code on each table or at the till ends that entirely, and unlike a menu code, customers universally appreciate it.

Set this up on a guest network, not your main one. Your till system, back-office computer and card terminal should not share a network with every customer's phone. Nearly all modern routers can broadcast a separate guest SSID with client isolation.

Generate it with our WiFi QR code generator, then print the password as text underneath the code as a fallback.

2. Google review link — the highest commercial return

This is the one most venues under-use. A code at the till or on the receipt pointing directly at your Google review form removes the friction that stops satisfied customers from leaving a review.

Get your review link from your Google Business Profile, then generate a static URL QR code for it. Place it where a customer stands after a good experience — at the till when paying, or on the receipt tray. Not on the table at the start of the meal.

Do not incentivise reviews with discounts. Google's policies prohibit it and it can affect your listing.

3. Menu — useful, but only done properly

Menu codes earned a poor reputation because of how they were implemented, not because the idea is bad. The mistakes were consistent: linking to a PDF, removing physical menus entirely, and hiding the code somewhere awkward.

If you do it, do it like this:

  • Link to a mobile web page, never a PDF. Pinching and zooming a PDF on a phone is genuinely unpleasant and customers will simply ask for a physical menu.
  • Keep physical menus available. The code should be a convenience, not a replacement. Older customers and anyone with a flat battery still need paper.
  • Make the page load fast. Customers on mobile data in a busy room will not wait for a heavy page.

The genuine benefit is price changes without reprinting — which for a venue changing specials weekly is substantial.

4. Short instructions — the quiet win

A text QR code needs no internet at all, which makes it useful for things a customer might scan before connecting: opening hours, allergen contact procedure, the WiFi terms, or table service instructions.

What not to do

Do not make the QR code the only way to do something. Every study of hospitality QR adoption points the same direction: codes work well as an option and badly as a requirement. Ordering-only-by-QR in particular generates complaints, excludes customers, and increases staff interruptions rather than reducing them.

Other reliable mistakes:

  • Printing too small to fit a table-talker design. A seated customer scans from around 40cm at an angle. Below 3cm you will get failures.
  • Glossy lamination. Evening lighting reflects off gloss straight into the camera. Matte solves it.
  • Codes on the underside of the table or on the wall behind the customer. Flat, visible, well-lit surfaces only.
  • Not testing after a redesign. Designers routinely crop away the quiet zone, which breaks scanning entirely.

Static or dynamic for a venue?

For WiFi, static is the only sensible option — a redirect cannot work for a customer who is not yet online.

For a menu, static pointing at a page you control is usually best: you change the menu page, the code stays the same. That gives you editable content without a monthly subscription and without depending on a third-party service that could shut down and kill every table card at once.

For review links and social profiles, static again — those addresses do not change.

The full trade-off is in our guide on static versus dynamic QR codes.

A sensible rollout

  1. Set up the guest network on your router, separate from the till and back office.
  2. Generate the WiFi code and test it on a phone that has forgotten the network — a phone already connected will appear to work regardless.
  3. Print one test card at final size on the actual stock. Scan it from a seated position, in evening lighting, on an ordinary phone.
  4. Roll out to two tables first for a week. Watch what customers actually do.
  5. Then print the full run, and add the review code at the till.

Test-printing one card before ordering a hundred is the step that saves the most money and the most frustration.

Maintenance nobody plans for

  • WiFi password changes. The password is inside the image, so a change means reprinting. Use card holders you can slide a new print into rather than rigid lamination.
  • Menu URL changes. If you rebuild your website, make sure the old menu path still works — otherwise every table card breaks at once.
  • Wear and tear. Table cards get spilled on. Check them monthly; a code with a coffee stain across a corner may still scan, but one with a scratch through a corner marker will not.
